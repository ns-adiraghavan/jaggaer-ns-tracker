Sustainability Needs for Manufacturing in 2026

3-4 min read | PublishedOctober 2026 | Published ByJason Rydzewski

In 2026, manufacturers must back every sustainability claim with verifiable data, especially from suppliers. Regulators, customers and investors now want to see how impact is measured, where supply chain risk sits, and whether the numbers hold up. As Andrew Roszko, CEO of JAGGAER, writes in Inside Supply Management, reporting once meant favorable statistics, a glossy report and a pledge to improve.

This shift away from glossy reports is most visible in procurement, where "sustainability commitments are only as good as the data behind them." To meet that standard, manufacturers must track new rules, assess double materiality, secure Scope 3 data, measure consistently and govern AI.

The 2026 Rulebook

What's changed for manufacturers in 2026?

Manufacturers now face new rules on green claims, carbon border costs, emissions disclosure and CSRD scope, all requiring verifiable data.

Generic environmental claims that cannot be substantiated have been prohibited under EU consumer law since September 27. Carbon estimates now cost more under the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM), which raises most default emission values by up to 30% by 2028.

Scope 1 and 2 disclosures are due November 10, 2026, for companies with revenue above $1 billion under California's SB 253. Scope 3 disclosure, covering the hardest-to-measure value-chain emissions, follows in 2027. The Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD), which mandates double materiality assessments, now applies only to companies with more than 1,000 employees and €450 million in turnover.

Two Lenses

Double Materiality for Manufacturers

Double materiality requires a company to assess two things: financial materiality and impact materiality.

Financial materiality measures how sustainability risks and opportunities could affect the company's financial performance. Impact materiality measures how its operations and supply chain affect society and the environment. CSRD requires both assessments under the European Sustainability Reporting Standards.

For an automotive components manufacturer, the two assessments cover:

Greenhouse gas emissions from the plant and supply chainExposure to EU fleet CO2 targets and fines

Labor conditions at Tier-2 and Tier-3 suppliers, such as battery-metal miningRaw material price volatility

Use the assessment to align investment with sustainability goals. Focus on the risks and impacts that matter to your footprint and industry, "rather than chasing vanity metrics," as Roszko advises.

Supplier Data

Getting Scope 3 Data From Suppliers

Manufacturers obtain reliable Scope 3 data by embedding supplier reporting into procurement, rather than reconstructing it after the fact. This requires 3 practices:

  1. 1Standardize the emissions data requested from suppliers at onboarding.
  2. 2Track emissions and labor-practice disclosures through ongoing supplier scorecards, not annual surveys.
  3. 3Reconcile supplier-reported figures against the spend and category data held in the source-to-pay platform.

Scope 3 emissions originate in the value chain and usually represent the largest share of a company's footprint. In 2023, BCG and CDP found that reported supply chain emissions averaged 26 times direct operational emissions. They are also notoriously difficult to track. Only one in five suppliers provide primary carbon data, and 30% provide none.

Five Years of Data

What does consistent measurement actually look like?

Consistent measurement means using a methodology that holds up year after year, through changes in scope, staff and business growth. A single measurement is easy; sustaining comparable disclosure over multiple years is the harder discipline.

JAGGAER has measured and published its greenhouse gas emissions for five consecutive years, using a methodology refined with Watershed. Total emissions fell 42% between 2021 and 2025, from 14,224 to 8,298 tonnes of CO2e.

Scope 1 and 2 emissions fell 23%, while Scope 3 emissions fell 43%, already exceeding JAGGAER's 2030 SBTi-aligned target. Scope 3 makes up over 90% of JAGGAER's footprint, with purchased goods and services the largest source. Most of the reduction therefore came from the value chain and not from JAGGAER's own operations.

Governing AI

AI governance and ESG Data are now part of sustainability

AI governance is now part of sustainability because AI increasingly shapes supplier selection, spend decisions and risk flags. Its outputs depend on its inputs. Andrew Roszko says "a supplier risk score or spend forecast is only as trustworthy as the data behind it". An unmanaged or opaque AI system can therefore create serious ethical risk.

3 frameworks guide responsible use: ISO/IEC 42001 for AI management systems, the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, and the EU AI Act. JAGGAER was the first source-to-pay company certified to ISO/IEC 42001 for its artificial intelligence management system.

Frameworks ISO/IEC 42001 NIST AI Risk Management Framework EU AI Act

Thus, treat guardrails against data breaches and rogue AI agents as part of the ESG agenda, not only as an IT concern.

Beyond Tier 1

Visibility Beyond Tier 1 Is the Foundation

The discipline worth copying is continuous monitoring and updating of reporting instead of chasing headline numbers. Reporting must adapt as new technologies, threats and opportunities emerge. 48% of buyers now have visibility of ESG practices for most of their Tier 1 suppliers, but it drops sharply at Tier 2 and beyond. Supplier visibility beyond Tier 1 is the foundation for disciplined, transparent reporting.
