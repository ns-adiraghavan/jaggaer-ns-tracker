Why Category Management Needs a Strategy

Category management needs a strategy because efficient sourcing delivers little when it chases the wrong goals. A category management strategy sets those goals for each spend category. It defines what the category must deliver, which risks it carries and which levers to use.

That direction matters more as resources tighten. The Hackett Group projects procurement workloads will rise 8% in 2026, even as headcount and operating budgets decline. Teams must also pull together. Yet 57% of CPOs in Deloitte’s 2025 Global CPO Survey ranked siloed ways of working among their top barriers to delivering value.

What Is a Category Management Strategy?

A category management strategy sets long-term direction for a spend category. It is a multiyear plan that aligns how the category is bought and governed with business goals, demand, supply markets and risk. Sourcing events help execute it, but they are not the strategy itself.

A category strategy differs from tactical sourcing in 4 ways. Tactical sourcing optimizes the next contract, while the strategy shapes the category over several years. Scope widens too, covering demand, specifications and supplier relationships rather than price alone. Input comes from business owners, finance, legal and operations, not just procurement. Value is measured broadly, from total cost of ownership to resilience, compliance and sustainability.

Total cost of ownership, resilience, compliance and sustainability

CIPS describes category management as running each category like an independent business unit, with its own strategy and goals. That makes the strategy the working link between business priorities and day-to-day procurement decisions. For a closer comparison, see category management vs strategic procurement.

Category Management Frameworks and Maturity Models

3 frameworks help teams build that link: the classic lifecycle, maturity models and portfolio segmentation. Each answers a different question, so mature teams combine them rather than follow one rigidly. Category strategy software lets teams apply all 3 in one place.

The Classic Category Management Framework

The lifecycle sets the order of work. Teams analyze spend and demand, then study the supply market. They develop the strategy, execute it through sourcing and contracts, and review results. The CIPS formalizes this as a cycle of 4 phases and 6 key steps. Its strength is discipline: strategy rests on evidence and gets refreshed rather than written once. For each stage in depth, see the category management process.

Category Management Maturity Models

Maturity models show how far a team has progressed. They trace a path from tactical, transaction-focused buying to strategic category management, then to value-led approaches. At each step, strategies grow longer-term, more data-driven and more closely tied to business goals and risk appetite. Bringing more spend under management is one marker of progress. Category Portfolio Approaches

Portfolio approaches decide where to focus attention. The best known is the Kraljic matrix, introduced in Harvard Business Review in 1983. It ranks categories by profit impact and supply risk, sorting them into strategic, leverage, bottleneck and routine. Each quadrant needs a different approach, from partnership for strategic categories to streamlined buying for routine ones. Many teams now extend it with regulatory exposure and ESG risk, which the original 2 axes do not capture.

Purpose: What Should the Category Deliver?

Purpose defines what success looks like for the business, beyond procurement metrics. Cost still leads CPO agendas, but rarely alone. In Deloitte’s 2025 Global CPO Survey, 72% of CPOs prioritized margins through cost reduction, 68% operational efficiency and 63% risk management. Purpose decides how those goals rank for each category. It also records non-negotiables set by regulation, brand or risk appetite, so trade-offs are made deliberately.

Portfolio: What Is in Scope and How Is Demand Structured?

Portfolio defines what the category covers and how it is consumed. Teams map demand by business unit, region and specification, often starting with spend analytics. That view shows where volumes can be aggregated, specifications standardized or suppliers consolidated. It also decides whether the category runs as one global strategy or splits into segments. Category management dashboards then keep that picture current as demand shifts.

Position: Where Does the Organization Stand in the Supply Market?

Position tests the strategy against external reality. It weighs supplier power, capacity limits, substitution options and exposure to regulatory, geopolitical and ESG risk. The answer shows where the organization has leverage and where it depends on suppliers. Where dependence is high, resilience or partnership may matter more than competition. Deloitte found 74% of CPOs count active alternative sources among their most effective risk strategies. Supplier intelligence keeps the assessment current as markets move.

Plan: How Will the Strategy Be Delivered and Governed?

Plan turns intent into action. It selects sourcing levers, such as tenders or renegotiation, and non-sourcing levers, such as demand management or supplier development. It also sets owners, timelines, approval paths and KPIs. Those controls let the strategy be monitored and refreshed rather than shelved. Examples

Category Management Strategy Examples: IT Services and MRO

These 2 category management strategy examples run the 4 Ps on very different categories. IT services is knowledge-intensive and often global. MRO is fragmented, low in unit value and critical to operations.

Example 1: The 4 Ps Applied to IT Services

IT services covers application development, infrastructure support and managed services. Gartner forecasts worldwide IT services spending will reach $1.57 trillion in 2026, up 5.3%. At that scale, small rate leaks add up fast.

$1.57T

Forecast worldwide IT services spending in 2026, up 5.3%

Source · Gartner

The 4 PsIT services
PurposeSecure scarce skills and reliable delivery while controlling total cost.
PortfolioSpend is scattered across many vendors, with opaque rate cards and scope creep through change requests.
PositionSpecialist skills are scarce, so leading providers hold pricing power, and switching mid-project is costly.
PlanRationalize demand, tier suppliers into strategic partners and niche specialists, and move to outcome-based contracts with rate benchmarks.
OutcomesFewer, stronger partners, transparent rates and more predictable project delivery.

Example 2: MRO Category Management Through the 4 Ps

MRO category management covers maintenance, repair and operations supplies, from spare parts to tools and consumables. Its biggest risk is downtime. Fluke commissioned a survey of 600 respondents in the US, UK and Germany. It found that 61% of manufacturers suffered unplanned downtime in the past year, which costs manufacturers up to $852 million a week.

The 4 PsMRO
PurposeKeep assets running at the lowest total cost, not the lowest unit price.
PortfolioThousands of low-value items, many suppliers and frequent maverick buying outside contracts.

Category Strategy by Industry

Category strategy follows the same 4 Ps in every industry, but each industry weights them differently. Chemicals lead with Position, energy and utilities with Plan, and pharma and life sciences with Purpose.

Chemicals and Materials

In chemicals and materials, Position comes first because feedstock access drives both cost and continuity. Deloitte’s 2027 Chemical Industry Outlook reports that Middle East disruptions in 2026 constrained Gulf-linked feedstock flows. Polypropylene, methanol and ammonia were among the chemicals affected. Deloitte expects commodity producers to respond with dual sourcing, regional production, tolling and alternate logistics routes. Qualification and regulatory requirements also slow substitution for specification-critical inputs. That makes supply optionality the core of any chemicals category strategy.

Deloitte expectsDual sourcingRegional productionTollingAlternate logistics routes

Energy and Utilities, Including Oil and Gas

Energy and utilities lean on Plan, because asset-intensive MRO, regulated spend and long contract cycles lock in decisions for years. Grid equipment shows the stakes. Wood Mackenzie projected 2025 US supply deficits of 30% for power transformers and 10% for distribution transformers. Imports were expected to cover about 80% of power transformer supply. In oil and gas, Deloitte’s 2026 outlook warned that tariffs could raise material and service costs by 4% to 40%. It expected operators to recontract with escalation, change-in-law and force majeure clauses. Utility category management therefore secures capacity early and writes volatility into contract terms.

Pharma, Life Sciences and R&D

In pharma and life sciences, Purpose carries the non-negotiables. Quality, regulatory compliance and supply continuity outrank unit price, because one failed input can halt production. USP’s 2025 Annual Drug Shortages Report found US drug shortages fell 23% to 75, yet now last 5.3 years on average. Upstream concentration adds risk: 33 of those 75 drugs rely on a key starting material made in only one country. R&D adds a Portfolio challenge, because research demand is fragmented across labs, projects and specialist suppliers. Pharma category strategies must secure the first and simplify the second.

Execution

Connecting Category Strategy to Sourcing and Execution

A category strategy creates value only when it shapes sourcing events, contracts and supplier management. Every tender, contract and supplier review should trace back to the Plan.

How Strategy Shapes Sourcing Events and Contracts

The strategy decides which events run, when and with what intent. A leverage category may go to competitive tender, while a strategic one moves to a long-term partnership. Contract terms then lock the strategy in, from pricing models to performance clauses. Category management software keeps that link visible from strategy to award.

Aligning Sourcing Tactics With Category Objectives

Tactics should follow category objectives, not habit. If resilience is the goal, dual sourcing can matter more than a single-supplier price cut. Where innovation matters, supplier selection weighs capability alongside cost.

Governance, Compliance and Stakeholder Alignment

Negotiated value leaks without governance. In one McKinsey example, an invoice-to-contract check built in 4 weeks found more than $10 million in value leakage. That finding led to supplier renegotiations. Regular reviews with finance, legal and business owners keep compliance high and the strategy current.

From Savings to Value

A strong category strategy measures value beyond savings. Total cost of ownership captures lifecycle costs that unit price hides. Supplier innovation can improve products and processes. Resilience lowers the cost of disruption, and ESG commitments shape which suppliers are chosen. Because Purpose set these goals, KPIs should track each one alongside savings.

Conclusion

Conclusion: Category Management Strategy as a Multiplier

A category management strategy multiplies the impact of every sourcing event, contract and supplier relationship. Without one, category management becomes a series of transactions. With one, each decision ties back to business goals, demand, market reality and a governed plan.

Most strategies fail in predictable ways. Teams treat the strategy as a one-time document, chase savings alone or build it without business stakeholders. Others skip supply market analysis or rely on incomplete spend data. Each gap breaks one of the 4 Ps.

The fix is to treat category strategy as a living discipline. Markets, regulation and demand shift, as the industry examples show. Strategies should be reviewed on a set cadence and refreshed when conditions change, not filed away until the next contract cycle. Leaders back that discipline with tools. FAQs

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a category management strategy?

+

A category management strategy is a multiyear plan that sets long-term direction for one spend category. It aligns how the category is bought and governed with business goals, demand, supply markets and risk.

What are the 4 Ps of category management strategy?

+

The 4 Ps of procurement category management strategy are Purpose, Portfolio, Position and Plan. They set category goals, scope demand, test the supply market and govern execution. They differ from the retail 4 Ps.

What is an example of a category management strategy?

+

IT services is a clear category management strategy example. Applying the 4 Ps, the strategy secures scarce skills, consolidates fragmented vendors, counters provider pricing power and shifts to outcome-based contracts.

What is MRO category management?

+

MRO category management strategically manages maintenance, repair and operations supplies such as spare parts, tools and consumables. It aims to keep assets running at the lowest total cost, not the lowest unit price.

How is a category management strategy different from strategic sourcing?

+

A category management strategy sets multiyear direction for a whole spend category, while strategic sourcing selects suppliers and contracts for specific requirements. Strategic sourcing is one lever a category strategy uses to deliver its plan.
