Supply Chain Risk Management: Steps, Supplier Risk and Solutions

12 min read | Published: October 2021 | Updated: November 2026 | By Visnja Radosavljevic

According to McKinsey’s 2025 supply chain survey, 95% of respondents have Tier 1 risk visibility, yet only 42% have visibility at Tier 2 or beyond.

That gap is where disruption hides. A failure at a Tier 2 or Tier 3 supplier still stops a production line, even when every Tier 1 supplier scores green.

Supply chain risk management closes the visibility gap with a repeatable cycle: map, assess, prioritize, mitigate and monitor. The golden rules of supplier risk management set the baseline for each stage of that cycle.

What Is Supply Chain Risk Management?

Supply chain risk management is the ongoing process of identifying, assessing, mitigating and monitoring threats to the flow of goods, services, data and cash across a supplier network. The scope runs from direct suppliers to the sub-tier suppliers behind them. It also covers logistics providers and any vendor with access to company systems.

Teams often use the term interchangeably with 3 others. Each has a different scope and a different owner:

Term Scope Typical owner
SCRM The full supply network, including logistics, geography and sub-tiers Supply chain and procurement
Supplier risk management Vetting, scoring and monitoring individual suppliers Procurement
Third-party risk management (TPRM) Vendor security, data and legal exposure Security, legal, compliance
Supply chain resilience The capacity to absorb a shock and recover Operations leadership

SCRM ties these disciplines together, so procurement, security, finance and operations work from one shared risk picture instead of separate registers.

Types of Supply Chain and Supplier Risks

Supply chain and supplier risks fall into 8 categories, and each category needs its own data and a named owner.

Risk category What the risk looks like Signals to monitor
Financial Insolvency, liquidity stress, off-balance-sheet debt Payment behavior, rating changes, financing structures, late filings
Geopolitical and trade Tariffs, export controls, sanctions Trade policy changes, restricted-party lists, country-of-origin exposure
Cyber and third-party Breaches or outages that start at a vendor Security posture, incident reports, system access reviews
Operational and quality Capacity limits, defects, factory incidents On-time delivery, nonconformance reports, capacity data
Logistics Chokepoint closures, port congestion, freight spikes Route exposure, lead-time variance, freight rates
Climate and natural hazard Storms, floods, heat, water stress Supplier site locations matched to hazard data
Regulatory and ESG Forced labor rules, emissions reporting, due diligence laws Certifications, audits, adverse media, questionnaires
Concentration Single-source, sole-source or regional dependency Spend share, qualified alternates, geographic clustering

Cyber and multi-tier exposure now lead the list for large US companies. KPMG’s 2026 US Supply Chain Survey ranked cybersecurity threats first, followed by multi-tier supplier risk. The survey covered 462 leaders at companies with $1 billion or more in revenue. Regulatory risks and supply shortages ranked close behind.

Concentration deserves special attention because concentration amplifies the other 7 categories. A sole-source supplier in a flood zone turns one local storm into a company-wide outage.

Why Supply Chain Risk Management Matters

Most companies still do not consider themselves ready for major disruption. The 2025 Resilience Consortium Pulse Check, reported by McKinsey and the World Economic Forum, found only 1 in 4 companies ready for major disruption across all resilience dimensions. The survey covered more than 270 executives.

Pressure is coming from several directions at once:

  • Trade policy: in McKinsey’s supply chain survey, 82% of respondents said new tariffs affect their supply chains.
  • Supplier finances: CLEPA’s Spring 2026 Pulse Check found 24% of European automotive suppliers expect negative profitability in 2026, up from 15% in the previous survey.

What Changed in 2025 and 2026

5 events show how quickly risk now moves through supplier tiers.

1

Export controls hit a sub-tier chip supplier (October 2025)

According to Nexperia, China’s Ministry of Commerce issued an October 4 notice barring Nexperia China and its subcontractors from exporting specific components made in China. S&P Global Mobility notes that Nexperia’s parts are essential for modules ranging from lighting and body electronics to airbag systems and powertrain controllers.

A single sub-tier supplier can still stop vehicle production when its parts sit inside Tier 1 modules.

2

A cyberattack at one manufacturer spread through supplier tiers (from late August 2025)

The Cyber Monitoring Centre modeled a £1.9 billion UK impact from the Jaguar Land Rover incident, affecting over 5,000 UK organizations. The modeled range ran from £1.6 billion to £2.1 billion. JLR suspended vehicle production for approximately 5 weeks across major UK plants.

The CMC also noted that JLR relies on nearly 1,000 Tier 1 suppliers and thousands of Tier 2 and 3 suppliers, and lacks direct relationships with many lower-tier suppliers.

3

A supplier’s financing structure hid risk (September 2025)

Bloomberg Law reported that First Brands filed for Chapter 11 with liabilities between $10 billion and $50 billion. The filing followed weeks of turmoil sparked by creditor concern over the company’s opaque off-balance-sheet financing.

A credit score alone does not reveal off-balance-sheet financing.

4

A shipping chokepoint closed (first half of 2026)

The World Bank described the Strait of Hormuz closure as the largest oil market disruption in history. Global oil supply fell by 10.1 million barrels per day in March 2026.

5

Breaches with third-party involvement approached half of all breaches (2026)

Verizon’s 2026 Data Breach Investigations Report found that breaches with third-party involvement rose 60% and now account for 48% of all breaches.

In every event, the trigger sat outside the buyer’s direct line of sight. Chips arrived inside Tier 1 modules. Debt sat off the supplier’s balance sheet. Breaches came with third-party involvement.

Steps to Manage Supply Chain and Supplier Risk

A supplier risk management program runs as a 5-step cycle, and each step produces an output the next step uses.

The events of 2025 and 2026 point to one recurring gap: visibility below Tier 1. A structured program closes that gap step by step.

Step 1: Map Your Supply Base and Set Risk Appetite

Step 1 of 5 · Map

Mapping starts with criticality, not spend alone. Segment your suppliers by spend, criticality and substitutability. For critical categories, ask Tier 1 suppliers to disclose the sub-tier sources and sites behind each part.

The Cyber Monitoring Centre’s JLR analysis makes the same point. A manufacturer without direct lower-tier relationships depends on its direct suppliers to support every key element of the supply chain.

Risk appetite defines which risks procurement can accept and which risks need executive sign-off. Assign one owner per risk category before scoring begins.

Step 2: Identify and Assess Supplier Risk

Step 2 of 5 · Assess

Supplier due diligence at onboarding sets the baseline, and scorecards track movement from that baseline.

Onboarding checks should cover:

  • ✓ Ownership and parent-company structure
  • ✓ Financial health and financing structures
  • ✓ Certifications and sanctions screening
  • ✓ Security posture

Scorecards then convert performance and risk data into comparable supplier scores. Use a supplier scorecard checklist to agree on criteria and weightings before automating any scoring. Weightings decide which suppliers surface first, so finance and operations should approve the weightings.

Step 3: Prioritize With a Risk Matrix

Step 3 of 5 · Prioritize

Not every risk deserves the same attention. A likelihood-impact matrix ranks each risk, and mitigation budget goes to risks that score high on both axes.

Score impact against criteria such as:

  • ✓ Time to recover from a supplier failure
  • ✓ Time to qualify an alternate supplier
  • ✓ Revenue and margin at risk
  • ✓ Number of customers affected
  • ✓ Number of approved alternates
  • ✓ Regulatory and reputational exposure

Sales and finance teams know which products carry the most revenue, so their input should set the impact scores.

Step 4: Mitigate Before and During Disruption

Step 4 of 5 · Mitigate

Mitigation has 2 parts: reduce exposure before an event, and respond fast during an event.

Before an event, the strongest proactive measures are:

  • ✓ Dual sourcing for parts with long qualification times.
  • ✓ Regional diversification where suppliers cluster in one country or hazard zone.
  • ✓ Targeted buffer stock for critical, hard-to-substitute items only.
  • ✓ Contract terms requiring disruption notice, sub-tier disclosure and business continuity plans.
  • ✓ Supplier development for strategic suppliers with weak financial or operational scores.

Applying category management for supply resilience builds these measures into each category strategy, so mitigation outlasts a single crisis.

During an event, speed depends on preparation. A crisis playbook names the trigger, the owner, the first actions, approved alternates and communication templates for each high-priority risk. In the opening days of a disruption, tighten purchase approvals and review force majeure and termination clauses.

Step 5: Monitor Continuously and Review

Step 5 of 5 · Monitor

Continuous monitoring converts a static supplier score into an early warning. Set alert thresholds by category and criticality across your supplier base, and route each alert to the named owner.

Build review into the operating rhythm:

  • ✓ Review the highest-priority risks every quarter.
  • ✓ Run a post-incident review after every disruption.
  • ✓ Feed the findings back into the risk matrix.

At each annual program review, check the program against the golden rules of supplier risk management.

How AI Supports Predictive Supplier Risk Management

Confidence in scaling AI across procurement remains low.

  • A Gartner survey of 101 CPOs, conducted in January and February 2026, found only 36% very confident in their ability to redesign roles and processes around AI.
  • In the KPMG 2026 survey, 7 in 10 supply chain leaders expect AI and GenAI to significantly reshape the supply chain workforce.

Continuous monitoring at scale is where AI earns its place. AI helps most in the repetitive, high-volume work of supplier risk. AI supports 5 supplier risk tasks, and each task keeps a human decision point:

Signal sensing

AI scans news and regulatory notices across languages and links each event to affected suppliers and sites. A buyer decides whether the event is material.

Sub-tier mapping

AI matches supplier names, parent companies and sites across systems. The category owner decides which sub-tier gaps to close first.

Early-warning scoring

AI combines performance trends, financial indicators and external events to flag rising risk. The risk owner decides on acceptance or escalation.

Scenario support

AI lists affected parts, open contracts and qualified alternates. The buyer decides whether to switch or exit a supplier.

Triage and drafting

AI summarizes alerts and drafts corrective-action plans. The supplier manager approves the final corrective-action terms.

AI output is only as reliable as the supplier master data behind the output. Require every AI-generated alert to cite its source data, and log every decision a buyer makes on an alert.

Procurement teams moving from reactive alerts to forecasting are adopting predictive supplier risk management.

Supplier Risk Management Solutions: What to Look for

Once the process and data foundations are in place, technology determines how far a program can scale.

The most useful supplier risk management solutions trigger action inside onboarding, sourcing and contracting, not only on a dashboard. A supplier risk management platform that stops at reporting leaves buyers to act manually.

Risk coverage Financial, geopolitical, cyber, ESG, operational and logistics risk in one supplier record Which risk categories are native, and which come from partners?
Multi-tier visibility Sub-tier suppliers and sites mapped for critical categories How is Tier 2+ data collected, validated and refreshed?
Data sources Named external data partners plus internal performance data Which third-party feeds are included, and how often do the feeds update?
Continuous monitoring Configurable thresholds with alerts routed to owners Can thresholds differ by category and criticality?
Workflow action Alerts trigger corrective plans, reviews or sourcing events What happens in the system after a threshold is breached?
Explainability Every score traceable to source data Can a buyer see why a supplier’s score changed?

Weight the 8 criteria by risk profile. A discrete manufacturer with deep electronics bills of materials needs multi-tier mapping first. A public sector buyer may weight audit trails higher.

Before full rollout, pilot any supplier risk management tool on a small group of critical suppliers. A supplier risk management solution passes the pilot when alerts lead to recorded decisions.

Frequently Asked Questions About Supply Chain Risk Management

Who should own supply chain risk management? +
Procurement usually owns supplier risk, while supply chain operations owns logistics and network risk. A cross-functional risk council adds security, finance and legal. Each risk category needs one named owner with authority to act.
How do you assess Tier 2 suppliers without a direct contract? +
Add sub-tier disclosure clauses to Tier 1 contracts for critical parts. Combine supplier-provided maps with external data on sites, ownership and events. Start with sole-source and long-lead parts, where a Tier 2 failure causes the longest outage.
What KPIs measure a supplier risk management program? +
Common KPIs include the share of critical spend with mapped sub-tiers, time from alert to owner response, and the share of sole-source parts with approved alternates. Track recovery time after each incident.
What should supplier risk management solutions include? +
Supplier risk management solutions should combine multiple risk categories in one supplier record. They should also support multi-tier visibility, configurable alerts, workflow actions and explainable scores. ERP and sourcing integration lets alerts change purchasing decisions.
Is supply chain risk management the same as third-party risk management? +
No. Third-party risk management focuses on vendor security, data and legal exposure, while supply chain risk management also covers logistics, geopolitics, climate and supply continuity. The 2 programs share cyber risk data.
