Ocean peak season 2026: why the import forecast keeps changing

5 min read | PublishedSeptember 29, 2026 | By

According to the National Retail Federation (NRF) and Hackett Associates, the ocean peak season is not over. Their September 9 report forecasts September imports at major U.S. container ports 9.6% above last year.

In July, NRF and Hackett had September imports 5.7% below last year. BUT the September forecast has since swung 15.3 points, from decline to growth, in 2 months. At the Port of Los Angeles, August capped the busiest 3 consecutive months in port history, which dates to 1907.

Peak season orders timed to the July forecast would have assumed a drop that the latest report no longer shows.

What changed in 2026: 5 forecasts for the same month

NRF and Hackett Associates changed their September forecast 4 times between May and September. The Global Port Tracker covers imports at major U.S. container ports, measured in twenty-foot equivalent units (TEU).

July 8, 20261.99 million TEUdown 5.7%
August 7, 20262.16 million TEUup 2.8%
September 9, 20262.31 million TEUup 9.6%

The net revision is 10.9 points from the May report, 11.8 from the June report and 15.3 from the July low. The August report said the early peak season was winding down, and the September report raised its September forecast again.

The July report forecast a record 2.47 million TEU for July, driven by retailers stocking up before expected tariff increases. According to NRF's September report, actual July came in at 2.3 million TEU. The August report had projected 2.21 million, so the forecasts missed in both directions.

As recently as August, May's 2.24 million TEU appeared to be the busiest month of 2026. NRF says high import levels continued and extended the peak season into its traditional late-summer and early-fall window.

The September report forecasts October at 2.11 million TEU (up 1.7%), November at 2 million (down 0.9%) and December at 2.03 million (up 1.1%). The 2026 total rose 0.2 million TEU between the August and September reports, from 25.5 million to 25.7 million. Versus the August report, July through September gain roughly 0.3 million TEU and October through December lose less than 0.1 million.

What the Los Angeles data shows

The Port of Los Angeles set a 3-month volume record this summer, BUT July and August were flat to lower than 2025. It handled 955,907 TEU in August, 6% above its 5-year average and on par with last year. Loaded imports reached 500,302 TEU, 7% above the 5-year August average. June through August totaled more than 2.9 million TEU.

According to the port's 2026 container statistics, June rose 12.37% from 2025, while July fell 5.82% and August fell 0.26%.

The tracker covers ports on the West, East and Gulf coasts, so Los Angeles is only part of the national figure. Executive Director Gene Seroka said the port may complete most of this year's peak season by the end of September. At the port's September 9 media briefing, he also said September was shaping up to exceed 900,000 TEU. Ocean peak season has historically run through the first or second week of November, he said.

NRF's September report puts traditional peak season timing in late summer and early fall, while Seroka's historical window runs into November.

What is extending the peak season

According to NRF, weather delays in China, Panama Canal rerouting and steady consumer demand help explain the extended peak season. NRF's Jonathan Gold said the rerouting came amid potential drought conditions. Weather delays and the rerouting, he said, explain some of the shift from earlier in the summer.

On demand, Gold said consumers kept buying despite tariffs, inflation and high fuel prices. Hackett Associates Founder Ben Hackett said imports stayed buoyant over the past 3 months and cargo is moving relatively smoothly. He noted reports of vessel delays and longer transit times.

Retail Industry Leaders Association (RILA) CEO Brian Dodge said retailers remain optimistic about the holidays. A large share of holiday merchandise is already in the U.S., he said, and more shipments will come as retailers replenish inventories.

Seroka estimated a 5% uptick in Los Angeles cargo over normal run rates. Some cargo that would otherwise move through the Panama Canal is being booked on West Coast services, he said. He also described vessel arrivals bunching after an active typhoon season in Asia.

Costs are a separate pressure. On September 9, Seroka said conflicts in the Middle East have forced carriers around the Cape of Good Hope. That adds 2 to 2.5 weeks of sailing time on affected services. Citing AAA, he said Southern California diesel prices were up more than 50%. In its September report, NRF cites high fuel prices as a headwind for retailers, not as a reason volume rose.

See peak season exposure before costs rise

A 15.3-point revision in 2 months can disrupt a peak season order plan built on a single external forecast. Your exposure follows your suppliers' locations and the routes their freight travels, weather included.

Dodge said at the same briefing that retailers can change shipping routes quickly, but they build sourcing decisions to last decades. In the near term, adjust purchase timing and routing. Supplier changes take longer.

Before the peak season ends
  • Check which suppliers ship through the routes in the news.
  • Confirm where qualified alternatives are already on file.
  • Review the categories you ordered early.

The forecast covers the market as a whole and says nothing about which of your suppliers carry that exposure.
