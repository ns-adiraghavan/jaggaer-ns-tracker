What Is Cost Efficiency? Definition, Examples and How to Achieve It

11 min read | Published: August 2025 | Updated: October 2026

Cost efficiency measures how much an organization spends to produce a given output or result. A process is cost efficient when it delivers the required output, at the required quality, with the least waste of money, time, and materials. Spending less, on its own, doesn't make a process efficient.

The pressure to get this right has rarely been higher. Deloitte's 2025 Global Chief Procurement Officer Survey of more than 250 CPOs ranks improving margins via cost reduction (72%) as the top enterprise priority.

But The Hackett Group's 2026 Procurement Key Issues Study projects procurement workloads rising 8% in 2026, even as headcount and operating budgets decline. Leadership is asking teams to deliver more with less. That is a cost efficiency problem by definition.

Key Takeaways

  • Measure cost efficiency as cost per unit of meaningful output, such as cost per invoice or cost per PO.
  • Cost effectiveness is a different question: it asks whether your spending achieves the intended outcome.
  • A lower unit cost isn't always better. Adjust for quality, rework, and risk before you compare options.
  • In procurement, the largest gains usually sit in spend visibility, on-contract buying, and invoice processing.

What Does Cost Efficient Mean?

Cost efficient means producing a required output with fewer resources than the alternatives, without lowering the quality that output must meet. The definition of cost efficient has 2 parts, and both matter:

Resources: Labor hours, purchase price, systems, overhead, and the cost of fixing errors.
Output: Something the business values: invoices paid correctly, parts delivered to spec, or requisitions converted to POs.

The cost efficient meaning applies to processes, suppliers, and decisions alike. A cost efficient supplier delivers usable goods at the lowest total cost, which isn't always the lowest quote. Likewise, a cost efficient invoice process pays more invoices correctly for every dollar of accounts payable (AP) cost.

A simple test helps here. Cheap describes a price, while efficient describes the relationship between cost and result. A low-cost process that creates rework, late payments, or compliance gaps is cheap, but it isn't efficient. Most procurement teams have inherited at least one process like that.

Cost Efficiency vs. Cost Effectiveness

The two terms are often used interchangeably, but cost efficiency vs cost effectiveness comes down to 2 different questions. Cost efficiency asks whether you're doing things right, while cost effectiveness asks whether your spending achieves the outcome you intended. One compares inputs with output. The other compares spending with results.

They can, and often do, diverge. A sourcing team can run RFPs at a very low cost per event and still award contracts that fail on delivery. That process is efficient but not effective. Meanwhile, a supplier development program that protects production schedules may be effective, yet expensive per unit.

In practice, the cost effective vs cost efficient question isn't either/or: you need both lenses. Efficiency without effectiveness optimizes the wrong work, and effectiveness without efficiency hits the target at a cost the business can't sustain. With the distinction clear, the next step is putting a number on efficiency.

How to Measure Cost Efficiency: Formula and Examples

The most useful cost efficiency formula is also the simplest. Divide total cost by units of meaningful output:

Cost per unit = Total cost of inputs ÷ Units of output

The hard part is the numerator. Total cost must include every input, not just the visible ones.

APQC defines AP cost per invoice as personnel, systems, overhead, outsourced, and other costs, divided by invoices processed. Apply the same discipline to any process you measure.

It's also worth noticing what credible benchmarks pair with cost. APQC's 2026 cross-industry AP benchmarks track cost per invoice alongside first-time error-free disbursements and invoice-to-payment cycle time. Cost is never reported on its own.

A current benchmark puts that cost in context. APQC's cross-industry benchmark puts the median cost to process an invoice at $6.00, based on 5,846 organizations. The Year 1 figure in the example below matches that median.

Worked example 1: AP cost per invoice (hypothetical figures)

MeasureYear 1Year 2
Total AP process cost$600,000$480,000

What the numbers show:

  • Cost per invoice fell 33.3%, from $6.00 to $4.00: ($6.00 − $4.00) ÷ $6.00 = 0.333.
  • Total cost dropped 20% while volume rose 20%, so both sides of the ratio moved in the right direction.

This is where many cost efficiency analyses go wrong. A lower unit cost doesn't automatically mean better efficiency. If quality, compliance, or outcomes slip, the cheaper option can end up costing more. To correct for this, divide the unit price by the acceptance rate.

Worked example 2: quality-adjusted supplier cost (hypothetical figures)

MeasureSupplier ASupplier B
Unit price$10.00$9.50
Units accepted at inspection99%92%
Cost per usable unit$10.00 ÷ 0.99 = $10.10$9.50 ÷ 0.92 = $10.33

Supplier B quotes 5% lower but costs about 2.2% more per usable unit. That gap also excludes rework labor, line delays, and return freight, all of which make it wider.

At the business level, a cost efficiency analysis often uses an operating ratio: operating expenses ÷ revenue × 100. A company with $45 million in operating expenses and $60 million in revenue has a ratio of 75% (hypothetical). A falling ratio means each dollar of revenue costs less to generate.

Before you trust a cost efficiency number, check 4 things

  • The output is something the business actually values.
  • Total cost includes error correction, not just labor and systems.
  • A baseline exists from before the change.
  • A quality measure sits next to the cost measure, such as first-pass match rate, on-contract spend, or error-free payments.

Cost Efficiency Examples in Business and Procurement

Cost efficiency shows up wherever an organization delivers the same result with fewer touches, fewer errors, or a lower total cost. These 5 examples come from the procurement and finance processes where the gap is easiest to measure.

1
Invoice processing

Manual keying and line-by-line review drive up AP cost. Automated 2- and 3-way matching sends only the exceptions to people. Track cost per invoice alongside first-pass match rate.

2
On-contract purchasing

An off-contract purchase can carry a lower sticker price and still cost more. Off-contract buying adds processing work and erodes the volume commitments behind negotiated rates. These tips to prevent maverick spend show where the leakage usually starts.

3
Supplier consolidation

Fewer suppliers per category means less onboarding, fewer invoices, and stronger volume pricing. Consolidation only pays off while concentration risk stays within tolerance. Teams that build savings through strategic supplier collaboration often keep a qualified backup for each critical category.

4
Early-payment discounts

Under 2/10 net 30 terms, taking the discount is worth an annualized return of roughly 37%. The calculation is 2 ÷ 98 × 365 ÷ 20. Take the discount only when your cash position allows.

5
Total cost of ownership

The machine with the lowest purchase price can carry higher energy, maintenance, and downtime costs. Compare lifetime cost per unit of output, not acquisition price.

For example: manufacturing MRO. Plant-level maintenance, repair, and operations (MRO) buying can fragment across many suppliers and price points. Catalog compliance at each plant, plus blanket PO call-offs tied to production schedules, keeps that spend on negotiated terms.

Knowing where waste hides is only half the job. The other half is a repeatable way to remove it.

Strategies to Minimize Operational Costs and Improve Financial Performance

The most reliable strategies to minimize operational costs and improve financial performance target waste in the process, not just the price on the PO. That focus matters more now because both targets and tools are moving.

What changed in 2026

Deloitte's 2025 survey found 40% of CPOs saw savings targets revised because of geopolitical developments, and 4 in 5 of those saw targets rise.

40% of CPOs saw savings targets revised due to geopolitical developments; 4 in 5 saw them increase (Deloitte 2025 Global Chief Procurement Officer Survey).

The tools are shifting too. The Hackett Group reports that AI-enabled technology entered procurement's top 3 priorities for the first time in 2026. Against that backdrop, 7 strategies consistently move the numbers.

1

Build a single view of spend

You can't improve cost efficiency in spend you can't see. Spend analytics harmonizes spend from every ERP into a single view, so you can spot supplier fragmentation, duplicate supplier records, and pricing inaccuracies. Start with your largest categories.

2

Enforce on-contract purchasing

Guided buying in eProcurement software steers requesters to contracted suppliers and negotiated prices. That protects the savings your sourcing team already negotiated.

3

Automate invoicing and exception handling

Automated invoicing matches invoices to POs and receipts, so AP staff work the exceptions instead of every document. Measure cost per invoice before and after.

4

Buy on total cost of ownership

Build freight, quality failures, maintenance, and switching costs into every award decision. The lowest bid should win only when it also wins on total cost.

5

Standardize before you automate

Automating an inconsistent process makes waste faster, not smaller. Align approval rules, coding, and tolerances first.

6

Pair every cost KPI with a quality KPI

Cost per invoice belongs next to first-pass match rate. Price variance belongs next to defect rate. Paired metrics stop cost cuts from hiding performance losses. Track cost efficiency at the category level, not just in aggregate.

7

Review payment terms and the supplier base every year

Terms, discount capture, and supplier counts drift without an annual review. Use the review to rebalance categories against risk.

Several of these strategies depend on the right systems, which is where technology comes in.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is cost efficiency?+
Cost efficiency is the relationship between what an organization spends and the output it produces. It improves when the same output, at the same quality, costs less to produce, or when the same cost produces more. Most teams measure it as cost per unit, such as cost per invoice or cost per purchase order.
What does cost efficient mean?+
Cost efficient means achieving a required result with the least waste of money, time, and materials. A cost efficient process or supplier isn't simply the cheapest option. It delivers the output the business needs, at the required quality, for the lowest total cost once you count rework, delays, and errors.
What is the difference between cost efficient and cost effective?+
Cost efficient describes how economically you produce an output. Cost effective describes whether your spending achieves the intended outcome. An invoice process can run at a low cost per invoice and still pay the wrong suppliers. A supplier program can protect supply effectively while costing more per unit than the alternatives.
How do you calculate cost efficiency?+
Divide the total cost of a process by the units of output it produces. For example, $480,000 in annual AP cost divided by 120,000 invoices equals $4.00 per invoice (hypothetical). Include labor, systems, overhead, and error correction. Then track a quality measure alongside it, so lower costs don't hide lower performance.

Conclusion

Cost efficiency is a ratio worth managing deliberately. Measure cost per unit of meaningful output. Pair it with a quality measure. Then remove the waste between the two.

Procurement sits closer to that waste than almost any other function, from fragmented spend to manual invoice handling. That puts procurement at the center of any cost reduction program. The teams that measure carefully, and resist chasing the lowest number on its own, are the ones that turn efficiency into lasting margin.
