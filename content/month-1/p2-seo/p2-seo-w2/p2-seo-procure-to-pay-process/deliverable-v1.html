The Procure to Pay Process: Steps, Common Breakdowns, and Choosing the Right Software in 2026

4–5 min read | PublishedSeptember 2026 | ByJason Rydzewski

The procure to pay process is the workflow that takes a purchase from request to final payment, covering requisition, purchase order, receipt, invoice matching, and payment. It connects procurement and accounts payable into one auditable chain, typically run in seven steps.

Knowing the steps isn’t where most teams get stuck. The real cost shows up at predictable breakpoints - non-PO invoices, missing receipts, and slow approvals - each capable of turning a clean process into a bottleneck. This guide covers all three layers: the seven-step workflow, exactly where it breaks down, and what automation changes at each stage, backed by sourced benchmarks.

It also covers what separates strong procure to pay software from a feature checklist, so evaluating a system is a deliberate decision.

Definitions

What Is Procure to Pay Process?

The end to end procure to pay process (P2P) is the workflow a company uses to acquire goods or services and pay for them, covering requisitioning, approvals, purchasing, receiving, invoice processing and matching, and payment. It connects procurement and accounts payable into a single, auditable process and it’s built to do more than move transactions along. Each stage creates a checkpoint: what is needed, what has been approved, what was ordered, what was received, what is owed, and what has been paid. That makes P2P a control mechanism as much as a workflow: the process defines the required checks, while software helps enforce them consistently. “Purchase-to-pay” is also commonly used interchangeably with “procure-to-pay” (P2P).

Procure to Pay Process Flow

A typical procure to pay process flow can be broken into seven stages, from identifying a need to completing payment. The exact sequence and number of stages can vary by organization and procurement system, but the following represents the common procure to pay process steps.

1
Requisition

An employee or department submits a formal request for goods or services, specifying what is needed and, typically, the required quantity, timing, and business justification. The requisition is routed for approval according to the organization’s policies.

2
Purchase Order

Once the requisition is approved and the supplier is selected, a purchase order (PO) is created and sent to the supplier. The PO specifies the agreed purchasing details, such as items or services, quantities, prices, and delivery terms.

3
Order Confirmation

The supplier acknowledges the PO, confirming agreed quantities, prices, and delivery terms before fulfillment. This step may be handled as part of PO processing rather than as a separate stage in some P2P workflows.

4
Goods or Services Receipt

The buyer records the delivery of goods or confirmation that services have been completed. The receipt record verifies what was actually received against what was ordered.

5
Invoice Matching

The supplier’s invoice is validated against the purchase order and, where applicable, the receipt. A two-way match compares the PO and invoice, while a three-way match compares the PO, receipt, and invoice. Mismatches can be flagged for investigation or resolution.

6
Invoice Approval

Once the invoice has passed the required validation and matching checks, it is approved for payment according to the organization's controls. Exceptions or mismatches may be routed for review and resolution before payment.

7
Payment

Accounts payable processes the approved invoice and issues payment to the supplier according to the agreed payment terms, completing the transaction and recording the payment in the financial system.

Where Things Go Wrong

Where the Procure to Pay Process Actually Breaks Down

Common P2P breakdowns occur around non-PO invoices, missing or incomplete receipts, and slow manual approvals. The overall workflow is well defined, but these recurring exceptions can disrupt it.

Non-PO Invoices

When suppliers bill for purchases made outside the approved purchasing channel, accounts payable lacks a PO-based reference for automated matching. This can increase exception handling, create processing backlogs, and make the audit trail harder to maintain.

Missing or Inconsistent Receipts

When goods or services aren’t recorded consistently, it becomes harder to verify that delivery or completion occurred. Invoice exceptions can increase, and payment may stall while someone confirms receipt.

Slow, Manual Approvals

When approval routing depends on email or ad hoc follow-ups, processing times become less predictable. A single unavailable approver can hold up an invoice until the required authorization is obtained.

Where Exceptions Compound

These issues compound one another. A purchase made outside the standard PO process may require additional documentation, while an unrecorded receipt can create another exception during invoice processing. When these exceptions enter an approval queue without clear ownership, resolution can slow further.

Many P2P breakdowns occur at specific handoff and accountability points rather than in the overall process itself.

Optimization

Best Practices for Procure to Pay Process Optimization in 2026

The best-performing procure to pay teams optimize the same three points where breakdowns happen: PO compliance, receiving, and approvals. Fixing these in sequence closes most of the gap between a documented process and an optimized one.

It starts before the purchase happens. Requiring a PO for repeatable spend categories keeps exceptions rare, and reviewing the exceptions that slip through monthly stops the same gaps from repeating. That discipline only holds if receiving is just as consistent. Clean PO practices and a well-maintained vendor file produce a lower exception rate than processes that lack both. According to HammerFin, teams with clean receiving resolve invoice errors in three days, against seven for bottom-quartile organizations.

Approvals are where these gains compound or stall. According to Ascend Software, more than 60% of invoices still require some level of human interaction, largely from manual routing. Threshold-based routing clears low-risk purchases automatically and gives stalled approvals a named owner. Centralizing POs, receipts, and invoices under one reference number turns three-way matching into a lookup instead of a search.

The payoff: according to APQC benchmarking, top-performing AP organizations achieve 98% of disbursements error-free on the first attempt, against 88% for bottom-quartile organizations. That ten-point gap is what these practices, taken together, are built to close.

What Changed in P2P in 2026

Three shifts define procure to pay this year: e-invoicing moved from optional to mandatory across major markets, matching logic got smarter instead of just stricter, and exception handling started shifting from a queue to a real-time routing layer.

E-invoicing mandates went live. Belgium’s domestic B2B mandate took effect January 1, 2026, Poland’s KSeF system phased in for large taxpayers that February and everyone else that April, and France’s mandate follows on September 1, 2026. For any organization buying or selling across these markets, structured, government-cleared e-invoices are now a compliance requirement, not a future item on the roadmap. (Finbite, E-Invoicing Mandates in Europe 2026)

Matching logic got more adaptive. Rule-based three-way matching checks an invoice against fixed tolerances and stops there. The AI-driven matching now reaching P2P platforms instead learns acceptable variance by vendor and category over time, which means fewer clean invoices get flagged just for falling slightly outside a static rule. (Medius, What Is 3-Way Matching in Accounts Payable?)

Exception handling is moving toward real time. Instead of routing every discrepancy into a shared queue for someone to triage later, more P2P platforms now flag only genuine exceptions and route them immediately, with the PO, receipt, and invoice already attached for context. It’s the same principle covered earlier in this guide, applied earlier in the workflow, before an exception has time to sit and compound.

Evaluating procure to pay software comes down to five criteria: ERP fit, automation depth, compliance controls, scalability, and analyst validation. Feature checklists alone won’t separate vendors as most platforms cover the basics.

Scalability

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between Procure to Pay and Order to Pay? +
The terms are used interchangeably across most of the industry, though some ERP and software vendors use ‘order to pay’ to emphasize the ordering and payment stages specifically.
Is Order to Pay the same as Order to Cash? +
No, they sit on opposite sides of a transaction. Order to pay is the buyer’s process for purchasing and paying suppliers, while order to cash is the seller’s process for fulfilling orders and collecting payment from customers. The two are mirror processes, not variations of the same one.
