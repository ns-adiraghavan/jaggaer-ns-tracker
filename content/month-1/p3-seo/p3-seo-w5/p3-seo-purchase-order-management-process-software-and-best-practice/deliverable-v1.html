What Is Procurement? Definition, Process, Types and Best Practices

About 22 min read | Updated: October 13, 2026

Procurement is the end-to-end process organizations use to source, buy, and manage the goods and services they need to operate. It includes identifying business needs, analyzing spend, sourcing and evaluating suppliers, negotiating and creating contracts, onboarding suppliers, and purchasing. It also covers receiving, invoice matching and payment, supplier performance and risk management, and measuring value.

Modern procurement goes beyond buying. It connects external spend and supplier decisions with wider business objectives.

The exact process varies by organization, industry, business model, regulatory environment, and type of spend.

Key Takeaways

  • Procurement covers the full lifecycle of acquiring and managing externally supplied goods and services. Purchasing focuses more narrowly on completing transactions.
  • Direct procurement covers goods and services that contribute directly to the products or revenue an organization generates. Indirect procurement covers the goods and services an organization needs to operate. Goods and services describe what you buy, and either can be direct or indirect.
  • A complete process runs from spend analysis and sourcing through contracting and onboarding. It then covers requisitions, approvals, purchase orders, goods receipt, three-way invoice matching, and payment. Supplier management continues throughout.
  • Source-to-pay (S2P) is the combination of source-to-contract (S2C) and procure-to-pay (P2P).
  • Measure performance across cost, process efficiency, compliance, supplier performance, resilience, and broader business value.
  • AI, automation, analytics, and connected source-to-pay technology are changing how teams analyze spend, manage suppliers, process transactions, and make decisions.

What Is Procurement? A Simple Example

The Chartered Institute of Procurement & Supply (CIPS) defines procurement as acquiring goods, services, or works from an external source. The definition covers identifying needs, soliciting bids, evaluating suppliers, negotiating contracts, and managing supplier relationships.

Example: A manufacturer needs a new packaging material. It analyzes its packaging spend, runs an RFQ, negotiates and signs a contract, and onboards the winning supplier. Planners then raise requisitions, approvers sign off, and the organization issues purchase orders. On delivery, the warehouse records a goods receipt note. Accounts payable runs a three-way match against the purchase order and invoice, then pays the supplier. The team later reviews the supplier against agreed service levels.

What Does Procurement Include?

Procurement includes the strategic and operational activities required to get value from external spend and supplier relationships. The scope starts before you buy and continues long after the product or service arrives.

Typical activities include:

Strategic sourcing and contracting (source-to-contract)

  • identifying and validating business requirements
  • spend analysis and demand analysis
  • developing category management and sourcing strategies
  • researching supplier markets
  • supplier sourcing through RFIs, RFPs, and RFQs
  • supplier evaluation and selection
  • negotiating commercial terms
  • creating and managing contracts
  • supplier onboarding

Operational purchasing (procure-to-pay)

  • raising purchase requisitions
  • approval workflows
  • creating and sending purchase orders
  • goods receipt and quality assurance
  • invoice matching
  • payments

Supplier management (ongoing)

  • managing supplier risk and performance
  • monitoring contract and policy compliance
  • measuring procurement and supplier outcomes

Supplier onboarding deserves particular attention, because it sits between a signed contract and the first purchase order.

Technology for supplier and contract management is a common weak spot. Among end-to-end tools, Hackett's 2026 study ranked contract lifecycle management (51%) and supplier lifecycle management (37%) highest for falling short of expectations.

A mature procurement function manages more than price. Supplier resilience, product quality, service levels, compliance, innovation, sustainability, speed, risk, and total cost all influence the value a supplier creates.

What Is the Difference Between Procurement and Purchasing?

Procurement is the broader discipline of planning, sourcing, contracting, purchasing, and managing suppliers. Purchasing is one operational component of procurement and focuses primarily on completing an approved transaction. Sourcing, the work of finding, evaluating, and selecting suppliers, is likewise part of procurement rather than a separate discipline.

What Are the Main Types of Procurement?

Procurement splits into direct and indirect procurement, and goods and services give a second way to classify what you buy. Direct and indirect describe how the spend relates to the business. Goods and services describe the nature of the purchase, and each can be direct or indirect.

What Is Direct Procurement?

Direct procurement covers goods and services that contribute directly to the products an organization manufactures or delivers, or to the revenue it generates. Examples include raw materials, components, packaging, and contract manufacturing. Direct spend usually ties to a bill of materials, so you can forecast it from production plans and projected sales.

What Is Indirect Procurement?

Indirect procurement covers goods and services required to operate the business that do not become part of the finished product. Examples include IT, marketing, facilities, professional services, and corporate travel. Many indirect categories involve large, recurring contracts, not one-time purchases.

For examples and a side-by-side comparison, read direct vs. indirect procurement.

What Are Goods and Services Procurement?

Goods procurement covers physical items, finished or unfinished, from equipment and raw materials to office furniture. It can be direct (raw materials for production) or indirect (office supplies).

Services procurement covers people-based and professional services such as consulting, legal support, IT services, security, and facilities repair. It can also be direct (labor involved in production) or indirect (on-site security). A statement of work, rather than a specification and bill of materials, often governs services, so scope, deliverables, and acceptance criteria need particular care.

What Is the Procurement Process?

The process runs from identifying a business need to paying the supplier and reviewing supplier performance. The number of steps varies. Published guides use anywhere from 4 to 12, and shorter versions combine several of the steps below. The process has 14 steps across 3 connected stages:

  • Source-to-contract (S2C): steps 1–7
  • Procure-to-pay (P2P): steps 8–13
  • Supplier management: step 14, ongoing

Stage 1: Source-to-Contract

1. Identify the Business Need and Analyze Spend

Every purchase begins with a clear requirement: what is needed, why, quantity or scope, timing, budget, technical requirements, quality standards, and business outcomes.

Strong teams don't define needs in isolation. They ground them in spend analysis, which shows what the organization already buys, from whom, and at what price. Spend analysis typically follows 4 steps:

1
Extract the data.

Pull spend data from ERP systems, accounts payable, purchasing cards, contracts, and other sources into one dataset.

2
Cleanse the data.

Remove duplicates, correct errors, and normalize supplier names, so that "IBM," "IBM Corp." and "International Business Machines" count as one supplier.

3
Categorize the data.

This is the most important step. Map each transaction to a category taxonomy. Use a standard such as UNSPSC (United Nations Standard Products and Services Code), a custom taxonomy, or a hybrid. Without consistent categorization, you cannot compare or consolidate spend reliably.

4
Analyze the data.

Teams look for consolidation opportunities, price variance, off-contract spend, and supplier concentration. A common method is the Kraljic Matrix. Peter Kraljic introduced it in a 1983 Harvard Business Review article, and it plots categories by profit impact and supply risk into 4 quadrants:

Profit impact ↑
Supply risk →

Poorly defined requirements create problems later in sourcing, negotiation, contracting, and supplier performance.

2. Develop the Sourcing Strategy and Research the Supplier Market

The team decides whether an existing contract or supplier meets the requirement or a new sourcing event is necessary.

Relevant questions include:

  • Does an approved supplier already exist?
  • Is there an active contract?
  • Can you consolidate demand?
  • What did the organization previously pay?
  • What supply, geopolitical, financial, or compliance risks exist?
  • How competitive is the supplier market?

Not every requirement needs a full competitive sourcing event. Effort should reflect the value, complexity, risk, and strategic importance of the purchase, which is where the Kraljic quadrant helps set the approach.

3. Supplier Sourcing (RFI, RFP, RFQ)

When sourcing is necessary, the team identifies potential suppliers and runs the most appropriate competitive process:

RFI (Request for Information): gathers market information and screens which suppliers are capable of meeting the need.
RFP (Request for Proposal): asks shortlisted suppliers to propose approaches and solutions for a more complex requirement.
RFQ (Request for Quotation): compares pricing against a clearly defined specification.

In Hackett's 2026 study, 78% of respondents report eSourcing at pilot or large-scale deployment. An eSourcing solution gives every supplier the same information, scores responses consistently, and keeps the process auditable.

There is no universal requirement to obtain 3 quotes for every sourcing event. Competitive-bidding requirements depend on organizational policy, category strategy, spend threshold, market conditions, and applicable regulations.

4. Supplier Evaluation and Selection

Supplier evaluation needs to extend beyond price where the category warrants it. Teams usually build a weighted scorecard before bids arrive, covering criteria such as:

  • quality and delivery performance
  • financial stability
  • cybersecurity and data protection
  • regulatory and policy compliance
  • sustainability and ESG performance
  • capacity and geographic exposure
  • innovation and service capabilities
  • total cost of ownership

Scoring against pre-agreed weights makes the award decision more objective and easier to defend in an audit.

5. Negotiate Commercial Terms

Negotiation establishes the commercial and operational conditions under which the parties will work together.

Negotiations can cover:

  • price and pricing mechanisms
  • volume commitments
  • delivery schedules
  • service levels
  • payment terms
  • warranties
  • liability
  • intellectual property
  • performance requirements
  • renewal and termination provisions
  • risk allocation

Strong procurement negotiation focuses on total value, not simply the lowest unit price. A cheaper supplier can cost more overall if poor quality, late delivery, weak service, disruption, or extra administrative work offsets the saving. Good negotiation also protects the supplier relationship, because it creates value for both parties.

6. Create and Execute the Contract

Document the negotiated terms in a contract that both parties approve and sign. Teams draft contracts from approved templates and clause libraries, have legal and the business review them, and store them centrally.

Good contract management doesn't stop at signature. The team tracks obligations, milestones, pricing, renewal dates, and expiry alerts so that the negotiated value reaches actual spend.

7. Onboard the Supplier

Onboard every new supplier before it receives purchase orders or payment. Supplier onboarding usually includes:

  • collecting company, tax, and bank details
  • verifying identity and banking information to prevent fraud
  • completing compliance, insurance, cybersecurity, and ESG checks
  • signing policies such as a supplier code of conduct
  • setting the supplier up in ERP and purchasing systems
  • connecting the supplier to catalogs, portals, or eInvoicing

Slow or fragmented onboarding can delay the value of a new contract and push users toward off-contract buying. A self-service supplier portal with automated validation can help shorten this step.

Stage 2: Procure-to-Pay

For repeat purchases against an existing contract, the process starts here, at step 8.

8. Create the Purchase Requisition

A purchase requisition is an internal request to buy. It is not a contract with the supplier.

The requisition records what is needed, the quantity, the preferred supplier or catalog item, the cost center, and the budget. Where a contract is in place, requisitions should draw on the negotiated terms through catalogs or guided buying.

9. Approve the Requisition

Every requisition moves through the approval workflow before the organization commits to a purchase. Approval rules may depend on spend value, category, cost center, location, risk, or other company policies. Automated routing keeps approvals fast while keeping controls in place.

10. Create and Send the Purchase Order

After approval, the organization creates a purchase order (PO) and sends it to the supplier. The PO is the formal buying commitment.

The PO typically records:

  • goods or services ordered
  • quantity
  • agreed price
  • delivery requirements
  • delivery location
  • payment terms
  • contract or supplier references

Modern eProcurement systems can automate requisition routing, approvals, PO creation, supplier communication, catalogs, policy controls, and audit trails. They can replace manual email and paper-based processes.

11. Receive Goods and Perform Quality Assurance (GRN)

The organization verifies that the supplier delivered the order and records the delivery on a goods receipt note (GRN). The GRN confirms the items, quantities, and condition received against the PO.

For physical goods, receiving teams confirm quantities, condition, specifications, and quality, often through inspection or quality management processes. For services, the business owner confirms the supplier met the agreed milestones, deliverables, or service levels. SAP environments often use a service entry sheet.

Record and resolve shortages, damages, and quality issues according to the contract.

12. Match the Invoice (Three-Way Matching)

When the supplier invoice arrives, accounts payable checks it against the order and the receipt. Three-way invoice matching is the standard control for physical goods. It compares the purchase order (what you ordered), the goods receipt note (what you received), and the supplier invoice (what the supplier bills).

If all 3 agree within set tolerances, accounts payable approves the invoice for payment. Resolve exceptions such as incorrect quantities, prices, tax information, or missing receipts before payment. Invoicing automation can run the match and route only the exceptions to people.

For the two-way and four-way variants, read three-way invoice matching.

13. Process the Payment

Once accounts payable matches and approves the invoice, the organization pays by the agreed method and within the agreed terms. Timing payments well helps working capital, and it can also capture early-payment discounts. Every step from requisition to payment should leave a clear audit trail for finance, tax, and compliance purposes.

Stage 3: Supplier Management

14. Manage Supplier Performance, Risk, and Compliance

Procurement does not end when you pay the supplier. Supplier management runs throughout the relationship and covers 3 ongoing activities:

  • Managing supplier risk and performance: tracking delivery, quality, service, cost, risk, innovation, and sustainability through scorecards and regular business reviews.
  • Monitoring contract and policy compliance: checking that purchases go through contracted suppliers at contracted prices and that suppliers keep certifications and obligations current.
  • Measuring procurement and supplier outcomes: comparing realized value against the negotiated value.

Performance reviews also provide evidence for contract renewals, corrective actions, sourcing decisions, supplier development, and future negotiations, which feeds back into step 1.

Pro tip: Define supplier performance measures during sourcing and contracting, not after performance begins to decline. Clear measures make supplier reviews more objective and give both parties a shared definition of success.

How Do Source-to-Contract and Procure-to-Pay Make Up Source-to-Pay?

Source-to-pay (S2P) is the complete procurement lifecycle. It combines 2 processes:

Source-to-pay = Source-to-contract + Procure-to-pay

  • Source-to-contract (S2C) covers spend analysis, category planning, supplier sourcing, supplier evaluation, negotiation, contracting, and supplier onboarding.
  • Procure-to-pay (P2P) covers requisitions, approvals, purchase orders, goods receipt, invoice matching, and payment.

Why Is Procurement Important?

Procurement affects how an organization spends money, which suppliers it relies on, and how much supply risk it carries.

An effective procurement function can influence:

Cost and value. It can improve commercial terms, consolidate demand, reduce unnecessary spending, increase contract utilization, and identify opportunities beyond unit-price savings.

Kearney's 2026 Assessment of Excellence in Procurement finds third-party spend is the biggest cost element as a share of revenue for most companies. It averages more than 60% in manufacturing and 30% in services.

As of October 13, 2026, APQC's all-company benchmark puts the median at 33.8% of revenue spent on purchased materials and services across 8,886 companies.

Supply continuity. Supplier selection, risk monitoring, alternative-source planning, and supplier collaboration reduce exposure to disruptions. See our guide to supply chain risk management.

In Deloitte's 2025 Global CPO Survey, 74% of CPOs named maintaining active alternative sources as the most effective risk-mitigation strategy. In the same survey, 64% prioritized greater supply chain visibility and 61% focused on supplier information sharing and collaboration.

Quality. Clear specifications, supplier qualification, contract requirements, and performance management create stronger control over quality.

Compliance. Policies, contracts, approvals, supplier due diligence, and audit trails reduce uncontrolled purchasing and make buying activity easier to govern. Read our procurement compliance guide.

Supplier innovation. Strategic suppliers can contribute product, process, technology, and operational ideas when supplier relationships extend beyond transactional buying.

Sustainability and responsible procurement. Teams increasingly influence environmental, ethical, social, and governance requirements through supplier selection, contracting, monitoring, and collaboration.

Business resilience. Strong supplier intelligence gives organizations a clearer view of concentration risk, financial exposure, geographic dependencies, capacity constraints, and other vulnerabilities.

KPMG's 2026 US Supply Chain Survey found 94% of 462 US supply chain leaders innovating in risk and resilience now or within 3 years. The leaders work at companies with $1B+ in revenue. Cybersecurity ranked as the top perceived supply chain risk, followed by multi-tier supplier exposure.

CIPS's Q2 2026 Pulse Survey found geopolitical risk and political uncertainty the primary concern at 35%. Cyber risk also became a more prominent concern.

Priorities continue to broaden. The Hackett Group's 2026 Procurement Agenda and Key Issues Study, featured by Jaggaer, ranks supply continuity first and spend cost reduction second. Deploying AI-enabled technology enters the list for the first time, in third place, and transforming the operating model ranks fourth. The study also finds workloads continue to rise as budgets and headcount come under pressure. Its survey skews toward large enterprises: 63% of respondents report revenue above $5 billion.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is procurement in simple terms? +
Procurement is the process an organization uses to obtain goods and services from external suppliers. It covers more than making a purchase. Procurement includes planning requirements, analyzing spend, sourcing suppliers, negotiating contracts, onboarding, purchasing, paying, and managing supplier risk and performance. It also measures the value of external spend.
What is the difference between procurement and purchasing? +
Purchasing focuses mainly on completing a buying transaction, such as approving a requisition, issuing a purchase order, and receiving the item or service. Procurement is broader. It includes spend analysis, sourcing strategy, supplier evaluation, negotiation, contracting, purchasing, supplier management, risk, performance, and long-term value creation.
Is sourcing the same as procurement? +
No. Sourcing is the part of procurement that finds, evaluates, and selects suppliers. Procurement also covers negotiation, contracting, purchasing, payment, and supplier management.
What are the main stages of the procurement process? +
A typical procurement process has 3 stages. Source-to-contract covers identifying the need, spend analysis, sourcing strategy, RFIs/RFPs/RFQs, supplier evaluation, negotiation, contracting, and supplier onboarding. Procure-to-pay covers requisitions, approvals, purchase orders, goods receipt (GRN), three-way invoice matching, and payment. Supplier management, the third stage, runs alongside both. The number of steps varies: published guides use between 4 and 12, and the process above has 14.
What are the 7 steps of the procurement process? +
A 7-step version groups the 14 steps as follows:
  1. Identify the need and analyze spend (steps 1–2)
  2. Source and evaluate suppliers (steps 3–4)
  3. Negotiate, contract, and onboard (steps 5–7)
  4. Requisition and approve (steps 8–9)
  5. Order and receive goods (steps 10–11)
  6. Match the invoice (step 12)
  7. Pay the supplier (step 13)
Supplier management (step 14) runs alongside all 7.
What is three-way matching in procurement? +
Three-way matching compares the purchase order, the goods receipt note, and the supplier invoice before accounts payable approves payment. If quantities and prices agree across all 3 documents, the invoice gets paid. If they don't, the team investigates the exception first. Invoicing automation can run the match and route only exceptions to people.
What is a GRN in procurement? +
A goods receipt note (GRN) is the record your receiving team creates when goods arrive. It confirms what the supplier delivered and its condition against the purchase order. It is one of the 3 documents in three-way invoice matching.
What is the difference between source-to-pay and procure-to-pay? +
Procure-to-pay covers the transactional stages from requisition to payment. Source-to-pay is the full lifecycle: it combines source-to-contract (spend analysis, sourcing, contracting, onboarding) with procure-to-pay.
What are the main types of procurement? +
Direct and indirect procurement are the 2 most common categories. Direct procurement covers goods and services that contribute directly to an organization's products or revenue. Indirect procurement covers goods and services needed to operate the business, such as software, facilities, professional services, marketing, travel, and office supplies. Goods and services describe what you buy. Many guides list 4 types (direct, indirect, goods, and services), and others list 3 (direct, indirect, and services).
Is procurement part of supply chain management? +
Yes. Procurement sits inside supply chain management, but they are not identical. ASCM defines supply chain management as designing, planning, executing, controlling, and monitoring supply chain activities. Its overview lists sourcing and procurement among the components. Procurement focuses on obtaining goods and services and managing suppliers. Supply chain management covers the wider flow of materials, information, inventory, logistics, production, and distribution from suppliers through to customers. Read more on procurement vs. supply chain management.
What does a procurement department do? +
A procurement department manages external spend and supplier relationships. Responsibilities include spend analysis, category strategy, sourcing, supplier evaluation, negotiation, contract management, supplier onboarding, purchasing governance, supplier performance, risk management, and compliance. The department also looks for opportunities to improve cost, resilience, quality, and business value.
What is a procurement job? +
A procurement job is a role in the function that buys goods and services for an organization. Roles range from buyers and sourcing specialists to category managers and procurement managers. Larger organizations add a chief procurement officer (CPO) who leads the function. Common skills include negotiation, analytical thinking, supplier management, and communication.
What is procurement software? +
