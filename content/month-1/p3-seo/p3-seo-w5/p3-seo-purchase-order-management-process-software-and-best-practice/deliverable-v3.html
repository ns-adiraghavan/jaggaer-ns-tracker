What Is Purchase Order Management?

PO management is the process of creating, approving, sending, tracking and reconciling purchase orders, from requisition to payment and PO close. It sets how you authorize, receive and match a purchase before payment. Accounts payable (AP) and treasury controls then govern the payment itself.

PO management answers 4 questions: who approved the spend, what the supplier committed to, what arrived, and whether the invoice matches. Each answer should leave a record an auditor can follow.

Purchase order management is the control layer within procure-to-pay, the cycle of requisitioning, ordering, receiving, invoicing and payment. For the distinction from procurement, read how procurement vs. purchasing differ.

The Purchase Order Management Process

The purchase order management process has 7 steps, and each step has a key control.

1
RequisitionThe requester states the need, quantity and cost center.Control: budget check.
2
ApprovalThe approver reviews the request against policy.Control: approval limit.
3
PO creationThe buyer issues the PO with supplier, price, quantity and terms.Control: approved supplier and price.
4
Transmission and acknowledgmentThe PO goes to the supplier through the agreed channel.Control: confirmed transmission, and acknowledgment where required.
5
ReceivingThe receiver records goods received or services accepted against the PO lines.Control: receipt or acceptance before payment.
6
Invoice matchingAP compares the invoice with the PO and the receipt.Control: tolerance check.
7
Payment and PO closeAP pays approved invoices. The PO closes when no commitment remains.Control: remaining commitment released.

Change orders cut across the 7 steps. Record agreed price and quantity changes on the PO before AP matches the invoice. Unrecorded changes become match exceptions.

Requisition

A requisition is an internal request to buy.

Purchase order

A PO is a formal document a buyer issues to a supplier, specifying the type, quantities and agreed prices of goods or services. It is a legally binding contract between buyer and supplier.

Roles and Responsibilities

5 roles typically touch a PO: requester, approver, buyer, receiver and AP. No one person should control authorization, receipt and recording of the same purchase. Where a small team cannot separate every duty, design alternative control activities, such as added management review.

GAO's 2025 Green Book, the federal internal-control standard, lists segregation of duties under Principle 10 (paragraphs 10.21–10.23).

Types of Purchase Orders

Match the PO type to how predictable the purchase is. Standard POs suit one-time buys, while blanket and planned POs suit repeat spend.

StandardOne-time purchase with known items, price and dateRisk if misused: Overuse for repeat buys multiplies PO volume
BlanketRecurring buys from one supplier up to a set limitRisk if misused: Needs a spend cap, release tracking and an expiry or review date
Planned or scheduledKnown total, with release dates still to comeRisk if misused: Releases drift from the plan unless reviewed
Contract-backedReleases against a negotiated agreementRisk if misused: Release prices must match the contract

Type names vary by ERP, so map these to your system's terms.

Two-, Three- and Four-Way Match

Match depth should follow risk and the evidence you need that delivery happened. Two-way suits low-risk buys, three-way adds a receipt or service acceptance, and four-way adds inspection results.

Two-way
PO + invoice

Low-risk or low-value buys, and services without a receipt record

Three-way
PO + goods receipt or service acceptance + invoice

Goods, and services with a recorded acceptance

Four-way
three-way match + inspection results

Buyers that inspect before paying

For services, the receipt may be a service entry, milestone acceptance or approved timesheet.

Set a tolerance so small variances do not trigger exceptions. Apply it by percentage, amount or both, and fail the match only beyond it. Cap percentage tolerances with a fixed amount.

Common Purchase Order Management Challenges

After-the-fact (retroactive) POs show where buyers bypass your purchase order management process. Track them by business unit, supplier and requester to find where the process breaks. One UK council shows the pattern. City of York Council's 2025 committee report counts an order as retrospective when its PO date follows the invoice date. Retrospective shares by department ran from 19% to 37% between January and June 2025.

Some retroactive POs are legitimate, such as emergency repairs. Log those as exceptions, not as the baseline. For the rest, match the fix to the cause. Options include guided buying and catalogs, simpler approvals, requester training, and a no-PO-no-work rule with suppliers for in-scope purchases. Not every purchase needs a PO, so document the exceptions: spend under an existing contract and low-value buys under a set threshold. The right fix depends on whether the cause is friction, policy gaps or habit.

5 more purchase order management challenges recur:

  • Manual entry errors. Wrong quantities and prices surface at invoice match. Pull PO lines from the approved requisition instead of re-keying them.
  • Approval bottlenecks. Stalled approvals push buyers to work around the PO. Set delegates and escalation timers.
  • Receipt and invoice mismatches. Late or missing receipts fail the match, so record receipts promptly and follow up on overdue ones.
  • No view of open commitments. Without purchase order tracking, stale open POs hide committed spend, so review them on a fixed schedule, such as monthly.
  • Change orders that never reach the PO. Price or quantity changes agreed by email break the match.

Purchase Order Management Best Practices

7 purchase order management practices give you a working control baseline. Each addresses a specific purchase order control gap.

  1. PO before purchase. Require the PO before the commitment. A retroactive PO cannot enforce the approval and budget checks it exists for. The Hackett Group's 2025 research reports top-quartile procurement teams lose 60% less savings than peers to maverick buying and contract noncompliance. That is a procurement-wide benchmark, not a PO-specific one.
  2. Approval limits tied to spend and role. Tie each limit to spend band and role, so approvals scale with risk, not availability.
  3. Segregation of duties, from the auditor's view. An auditor tests who performed each control and what evidence shows it. Keep approvals and receipts in the system, not in email threads.
  4. Tolerance rules for matching. Set tolerances by value and category, tight enough to catch errors and loose enough to ignore rounding differences. Ardent Partners' State of ePayables 2025 puts the average invoice exception rate at 18.4%. Best-in-class AP teams run 47% lower than the rest of the marketplace. That covers all invoice exceptions, not only PO mismatches.
  5. Catalogs for repeat buys. Catalog items carry approved prices and suppliers, so requesters stay on policy by default. Where low-value buying is scattered, add tail spend management solutions.
  6. Blanket POs for recurring spend. One capped PO replaces dozens of identical ones. Track releases against the cap and set an expiry.
  7. Regular review of open POs. Stale open POs overstate commitments and hide late deliveries. ACFE's 2026 report associates controls such as management review and proactive data monitoring with lower losses and faster detection.

FAQ

What is purchase order management? +
Purchase order management covers everything that happens to a PO between request and payment: approval, issue, receipt, invoice match and close. It provides the workflows and controls to authorize purchases, record receipt and match invoices.
What are the steps in the purchase order process? +
The purchase order management process commonly has 7 steps: requisition, approval, PO creation, transmission, receiving, invoice matching, and payment with PO close. Counts vary: teams often describe procure-to-pay in 5 stages, and some lists add vendor selection, which belongs to sourcing.
What is the difference between PO management and procurement? +
Procurement is the wider function: sourcing, contracting and supplier management. Purchase order management is the operational process that begins with the internal request and approval and runs through receipt, matching and close.
What is the difference between a purchase requisition and a purchase order? +
A requisition is an internal request to buy. A PO is the formal document sent to the supplier, and it is a legally binding contract between buyer and supplier. The purchase requisition vs. purchase order guide covers the full comparison.
What are two-way, three-way and four-way match, and when is each used? +
Two-way match compares the PO and invoice, three-way adds a goods receipt or service acceptance, and four-way adds inspection results. Match depth follows risk. Use two-way for lower-risk buys, three-way where you must confirm delivery, and four-way where inspection decides payment.
What are the different types of purchase orders? +
Common PO structures include standard, blanket, planned or scheduled, and contract-backed POs. Terminology varies by ERP.
Why do after-the-fact POs happen, and how do you reduce them? +
After-the-fact POs can happen when buying moves faster than approval, or when the PO process is harder than working around it. Reduce them with guided buying and catalogs, simpler approvals, requester training and a no-PO-no-work rule for in-scope purchases, with documented exceptions.
When should a company move from spreadsheets to PO management software? +
Consider purchase order management software when manual processes stop giving you control. Spreadsheets can track POs but typically lack integrated workflow, audit trail and matching.
