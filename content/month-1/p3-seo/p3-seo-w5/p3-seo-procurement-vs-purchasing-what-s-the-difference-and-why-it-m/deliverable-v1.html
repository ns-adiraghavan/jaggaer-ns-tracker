Procurement vs. Purchasing: What's the Difference in 2026?

5 min read | PublishedOctober 2026 | By Jason Rydzewski

A useful way to frame procurement vs. purchasing is decision versus execution. Procurement decides from whom to buy, and on what terms. Purchasing is the transactional subset that buys what procurement decided. Here it means the workflow from requisition through payment. Some organizations assign invoicing and payment to accounts payable. When the two connect, a negotiated contract shows up where the requester shops. When they do not, the decision does not reach the transaction, and negotiated value leaks in the gap.

That gap shows up in two ways. Either no contract exists for the category (a coverage gap), or buyers go around the contract that does (a compliance gap). Each needs a different fix, and fixing one does not fix the other. Fix coverage upstream, fix compliance across the buying process, and connect the two. As more transactional work moves onto software, the commercial decisions are what remain with your team.

What Is the Difference Between Procurement and Purchasing?

The Chartered Institute of Procurement & Supply (CIPS) separates the two by scope: procurement is the long-term approach, and purchasing fulfills the transaction. Procurement sets the terms across sourcing, contracts, and supplier performance. The full process is covered in the guide to what procurement is. Purchasing executes those terms from requisition to payment.

Procurement answers "why this supplier, at this price, under these terms?" Purchasing answers "how and when do we order it?"

Neither side of procurement vs. purchasing is the junior partner. A negotiated price saves money only when the order uses it. A clean requisition process, in turn, cannot fix a category that has no contract.

Procurement vs. Purchasing: Are the Two Terms Interchangeable?

In practice, the line between them blurs: titles do not consistently map to functions, and many professionals use the terms interchangeably. A purchasing manager can run strategic sourcing, and a procurement analyst can spend the week chasing purchase orders (POs).

The procurement vs. purchasing line only holds when you define it by what the work does, not what the title says. One test works well: does the activity set the terms, or execute against terms already set? Setting terms is procurement. Executing is purchasing.

Organizations split these activities differently, so procurement vs. purchasing is a functional boundary, not a rule about job titles. In some organizations, a procurement operations team sits between the layers and owns the systems and workflows that carry sourcing decisions into compliant buying. One team can do both. Problems arise when nothing connects the two halves of the procurement vs. purchasing handoff.

Procurement vs. Purchasing: Where Does the Money Leak?

Two sources of savings leakage are worth separating: no contract exists, or buyers ignore the contract. The maverick-buying measure from the American Productivity & Quality Center (APQC) captures the second.

As of late September 2026, live data from APQC showed median maverick buying at 1.2% of purchase value. APQC defines maverick buying as purchases that ignore an existing contract, so the measure does not capture categories with no contract. A median also says nothing about organizations at the high end of the distribution, whose maverick-buying rates sit above 1.2%.

The coverage leakUpstream

The coverage leak is primarily an upstream procurement problem. No contract exists for the category, the contract has lapsed, or the supplier was never sourced. Purchasing has nothing to buy against, so orders can go out at the price the supplier quotes.

The compliance leakAt the point of purchase

The compliance leak appears when buyers do not follow an existing contract. APQC's maverick-buying measure captures this one.

Here is how a compliance leak forms in the procurement vs. purchasing handoff. A sourcing team spends months on an award. Six weeks later, a requester in another department buys the same category from a supplier they already know. The preferred supplier is not in their catalog, and the approved route takes longer than a phone call. The purchase is legitimate, yet it bypasses the terms procurement negotiated.

APQC says maverick purchasing often occurs because employees see the standard buying process as too complicated or not worth the time. Three gaps can create that friction in the procurement vs. purchasing handoff:

  • Preferred items that are hard to find. The contract exists, but not where the requester shops.
  • Approval routes slower than the workaround. Buyers facing a deadline may contact a known supplier directly instead of waiting on multiple approvals.
  • Decentralized buying authority. Business units hold their own budgets and supplier relationships.

Part of the procurement vs. purchasing gap comes from process design, which makes it fixable. Design will not remove every bypass, but it removes the easy ones. The guide to preventing and controlling maverick spend starts with spend visibility.

What Should Procurement-Led Teams Do Differently?

Start by closing the coverage leak where it appears, because nothing can enforce a contract that does not exist. Then make the compliant path the easiest path. Track contract coverage by category, not only spend by supplier, and measure contract utilization and price compliance rates. Treat purchasing as the layer that carries procurement's decisions to the order, so the procurement vs. purchasing handoff works as one connected process.

The compliant path also pays back in speed. In APQC's 2019 comparison of organizations with at least $500 million in revenue, those sending 2% or more of purchases through maverick buying needed 16 more hours at the median to issue a purchase order than those under 1%. They also spent $2.58 more per $1,000 purchased on the procurement process. APQC links part of the gap to approved vendors: with vetted suppliers, procurement staff have established ways to issue orders quickly. The data describes one benchmark group and an association, not a guaranteed result, but it undercuts the assumption that the workaround is faster.

According to Deloitte's 2025 Global CPO Survey, improving margins through cost reduction and cost avoidance ranked as the top enterprise priority, at 72%. The open question in procurement vs. purchasing is how much negotiated value survives the buying process.

In Deloitte's survey, 96% of Digital Masters met or exceeded their cost-savings plans, against 80% of Followers. Digital Masters are top-quartile performers that also rank top quartile in demonstrated capabilities. Followers are the comparison group. Deloitte reported an association between technology and talent competencies and performance. The survey covered 250+ CPOs across 40 countries, and Deloitte noted it is not a formal procurement benchmark.

Deloitte found Digital Masters spend a 34% higher share of their time on strategic work than Followers. However, roughly two-thirds of their time is still spent on non-strategic work. Automating transactional work gives procurement teams more capacity for strategic work, and the Digital Masters' higher strategic share is consistent with that pattern. Because Deloitte reports an association rather than a cause, treat it as direction, not proof. For the procurement vs. purchasing handoff, the practical reading is to let software carry the transactional layer and keep the commercial decisions, such as contract coverage and the design of the compliant path, with the team.

What Changed in 2026: AI Became a Priority, Not Yet a Scaled Capability

According to The Hackett Group's 2026 Procurement Key Issues Study, 43% of organizations were actively pursuing AI deployment. However, only 12% reported large-scale implementation. The 12% is part of the 43%, and most organizations pursuing AI are still in pilots or single use cases. The 43% is nearly double last year's level. Early gains cluster in cycle time, productivity, and effectiveness.

Which Leak Are You Running?

Use these four questions as a quick heuristic, not a diagnostic, to test your own procurement vs. purchasing handoff. Two "no" answers in the first pair suggest a coverage gap, and two in the second suggest a compliance gap. Then investigate the weaker side first.

  2. 2Does every major category have an active contract?

Compliance questions
