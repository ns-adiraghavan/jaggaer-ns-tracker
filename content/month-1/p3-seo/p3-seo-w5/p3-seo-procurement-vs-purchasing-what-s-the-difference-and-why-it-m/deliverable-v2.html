Procurement vs. Purchasing: What's the Difference and Why It Matters in 2026

11 min read | PublishedOctober 2026

Procurement is the strategic, end-to-end process of acquiring what your business needs: finding suppliers, negotiating terms, and managing those relationships over time. Purchasing is the transactional step inside it: raising the requisition, issuing the purchase order (PO), receiving the goods, and paying the invoice.

The distinction matters because conflating the two usually hides a process gap. When nobody owns the upstream decisions (who you buy from, at what price, on what terms), orders become one-offs at whatever the supplier quotes. When nobody owns the transaction, the terms you negotiated do not reliably reach the person placing the order.

This guide covers how the two differ, how both fit into the procure-to-pay (P2P) cycle, and when informal buying stops working and it is time to formalize. You will leave with a one-line rule for telling the two apart and a checklist for judging whether your team has outgrown informal buying. CIPS draws the same line: procurement is a long-term approach to acquiring goods and services, while purchasing fulfills the transaction. Procurement covers sourcing suppliers, negotiating, contract management, and supplier development. Purchasing carries out the orders that result. Everyday usage is looser, and the two words are often used interchangeably, so check how your own organization uses them.

The two do not compete, and the difference between purchasing and procurement is scope and timing, not rank. Purchasing is one stage inside procurement, and each needs the other. A negotiated price saves money only when the order uses it. That is the procurement and purchasing difference in practice: a clean ordering process cannot fix a category nobody sourced.

If you are the person who both negotiates with suppliers and places the orders, you are doing both jobs today. That is why people search "is purchasing and procurement the same" and "what is the difference between procurement and purchasing". The question is whether the setup still holds as vendors and spend grow. To distinguish between purchasing and procurement in your own team, ask one question: does this work set the terms, or execute terms already set?

What Is Procurement?

Procurement is the end-to-end process of getting goods or services into your business, from spotting the need to managing the supplier afterward. The difference between procurement and purchasing starts here: procurement sets the terms before anyone places an order. The procure and purchase difference also shows in the verbs: to procure is to secure supply, and to purchase is to buy.

Four steps carry most of the work:

  • Identify the need. Define what the business requires, in what quantity, to what specification, and by when.
  • Source suppliers. Find and compare suppliers on price, quality, capacity, and risk. This is strategic sourcing.
  • Negotiate and contract. Agree on price, payment terms, delivery, and service levels, then record them in a contract.
  • Manage the supplier relationship. Track performance, resolve issues, and review the contract before it renews.

Done well, the procurement process can deliver cost savings, better quality, and lower supply chain risk, and CIPS ties effective procurement to all three. Sourcing is the second step, and how sourcing fits into procurement shows where it ends and contracting begins.

What Is Purchasing?

Purchasing is the transactional step of buying: you turn an approved need into an order, confirm what arrives, and pay for it. CIPS describes it as fulfilling the transaction, a reactive activity. It runs on terms procurement already set. The purchasing process has four steps:

  • Requisition. A requester asks for goods or services, with a cost and budget attached, and routes the request for approval.
  • PO issuance. Once approved, the buyer sends a PO that fixes the item, quantity, price, and delivery terms with the supplier.
  • Receiving. The business checks that what arrived matches the PO.
  • Invoice and payment. Accounts payable typically matches the invoice to the PO and the receipt, then pays on the agreed payment terms.

In a small company, one person often runs all four steps, which is why the line blurs and the procure vs purchase question comes up. If the first two steps run together for you, read purchase requisition vs. purchase order.

Purchasing vs. Procurement: Where They Fit Into Procure-to-Pay

In JAGGAER's definition, procure-to-pay (P2P) covers everything after a contract is in place: requisition, purchase order, receipt, invoice, and payment. That is the ground purchasing covers. Procurement's upstream work, sourcing and contracting, feeds it. Together they form the full sourcing-to-payment cycle, known as source-to-pay (S2P), with purchasing as one stage inside it. Procurement decides first, then purchasing carries the decision out.

The purchasing versus procurement confusion comes from the workflow itself. Because both stages sit in one connected process, teams often treat the words as synonyms. They are stages. Procurement's decisions shape the orders that follow, and purchasing is where those decisions either hold or get bypassed. Both sit inside supply chain management, because a late or off-contract order can disrupt what you can deliver. For where sourcing ends and the order begins, see how sourcing fits into procurement.

When the stages run on separate tools, the handoff breaks: the contract sits in one system, the requisition in another, and the invoice in a third. A clean handoff looks like this: the requester searches for an item, sees the contracted supplier and price, and the PO carries those terms through to payment. When Do You Need Dedicated Procurement Software?

You need dedicated procurement software when spreadsheets and email stop answering three questions: who approved this, who are we buying from, and what are we spending?

Every company starts with informal purchasing, and for a small team with a handful of vendors it works. The shift becomes necessary when vendor count, spend, or risk outgrows ad hoc buying. Four signs typically show up. Multiple approvers turn requests into email chains. Vendor sprawl puts the same item on several price lists. Spend stays invisible until the invoice lands. And maverick spend creeps in, where buyers go around contracts and approval rules.

Find where your team sits.

Signs informal purchasing still works
  • One or two people place every order, and they know each supplier personally.
  • You buy from a short list of vendors, and prices rarely change.
  • One approver signs off, so requests do not wait in a queue.
  • A single spreadsheet still shows what you spend with each supplier.
  • Almost nothing gets bought outside the process.
Signs you need procurement software
  • Requests stall because several approvers sit in the loop.
  • The same item comes from multiple vendors at different prices.
  • Finance sees spend only after the invoice arrives.
  • Buyers go around contracts or approvals (maverick spend).
  • Leadership or auditors ask for a trail you cannot produce.

Formalizing procurement starts with four moves, not software. List what you buy and from whom. Set approval rules by amount and category. Put contracts and preferred suppliers in place for your biggest categories. Route every request through one intake path. Software then applies those rules at the point of purchase, which helps them hold as volume grows.

When you evaluate software, test it against those failures. Look for four things:

  • Approval routing you configure to your own rules.
  • Catalogs that put contracted suppliers first.
  • One view of spend across requisitions, POs, and invoices.
  • An audit trail that records every exception.

Check fit as well. A growing company rarely needs every module on day one, so confirm you can start with requisitions and approvals and add sourcing and contracts later.

FAQ

Is procurement the same as purchasing?

+

No: procurement is the strategic, end-to-end process of sourcing and managing what a business buys, and purchasing is the transactional step of ordering and paying inside it. Purchasing and procurement are not the same thing, even though many teams use the words interchangeably and, in a small company, one person often does both jobs.

What's the difference between procurement and purchasing, in one sentence?

+

Procurement decides what to buy, from whom, and on what terms, while purchasing places the order and pays for it. The Chartered Institute of Procurement & Supply (CIPS) frames it as a long-term approach versus fulfilling the transaction.

What are the four types of procurement?

+

CIPS classifies procurement as direct (raw materials and goods needed for production) or indirect (purchases that support day-to-day operations), and its guide to what procurement is adds goods (physical items) and services (people-based services) as two more types. That gives four types, although CIPS's own types page lists only direct and indirect. Some other sources list capital purchases, meaning non-routine, high-value assets, in place of goods.

How does sourcing differ from procurement and purchasing?

+

Sourcing is one stage of procurement: finding, evaluating, and selecting suppliers, then agreeing terms. Procurement wraps sourcing together with contracting and supplier management, and purchasing starts once the sourcing decision is made and the order goes out. See how sourcing fits into procurement for where each line sits.

Who handles procurement vs. purchasing within a company?

+

Larger companies typically give procurement to category managers, sourcing leads, and a head of procurement, and give purchasing to buyers, requesters, and accounts payable. In smaller companies, one person, often an operations, finance, or office manager, covers both. The split matters less than being able to differentiate between purchasing and procurement: who owns the terms, and who owns the order.

What comes first — a purchase requisition or a purchase order?

+

The purchase requisition usually comes first. A requester files it to ask for approval to buy. Once approved, the buyer converts it into a purchase order and sends that to the supplier.

At what point should a company formalize its procurement function?

+

Formalize when vendor count, spend, or risk outgrows ad hoc buying. Typical triggers are several approvers on every request, the same item bought from multiple vendors at different prices, no view of spend until invoices arrive, and buyers going around contracts. If several of these sound familiar, define an approval path and start evaluating procurement software.

Does purchasing happen before or after procurement in the procure-to-pay cycle?

+

After. Procurement's sourcing and contracting decisions come first, and purchasing then carries them out through requisition, PO, receipt, and payment, which is the work procure-to-pay covers. On repeat buys, one contract serves many purchases, so procurement decides once and purchasing runs many times.
